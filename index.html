Sydney and Australia · October 2026 · Fear versus the numbers

The taxi to the airport is more dangerous than the flight.

5 kmBeyond 5 km by car, the trip to the airport carries more risk than a flight on an Australian airline. Sydney’s CBD is 8 km from the airport; Melbourne’s, about 23.

And the drive to the beach is more dangerous than the sharks. We fear what is rare and spectacular, and forget what kills every day. This site puts both on the same scale, using data from Sydney and the rest of Australia.

UnitThe micromort (micro + mortality): a one-in-a-million chance of dying. 0.01 micromorts is a one in 100 million chance. It is the standard unit in risk research, proposed by Ronald Howard of Stanford in 1979 and popularised by the statistician David Spiegelhalter.

The psychology of risk

Why we fear the wrong things

In 1978, Sarah Lichtenstein, Paul Slovic and colleagues asked Americans to estimate the yearly number of deaths from about forty causes. The answers followed a steady pattern: rare, dramatic causes were overestimated, and common, quiet ones underestimated. Even when warned about the bias, people could not correct it.

A year later, Barbara Combs and Paul Slovic showed that these errors closely tracked how much each cause appeared in newspapers, with correlations of 0.85 to 0.89. Our fears reflect what gets talked about, not what happens.

Availability heuristic

What comes to mind feels common

We judge how often something happens by how easily we can recall it. A plane crash leads the news for a week. The three or four people who die on Australian roads every day almost never do.

Dread risk

Sudden, collective, out of our hands

Paul Slovic showed that how frightening a risk feels weighs more than its probability. Plane crashes and shark attacks tick every box: a violent death, unpredictable, with nothing we can do about it.

Illusion of control

Behind the wheel, we feel in charge

Driving gives a sense of control that a plane cabin does not. Yet per kilometre travelled, cars kill about 100 times more people than airliners (United States, 2000-2009).

Ignoring the denominator

Counting attacks is not enough

"Three attacks in two days" is a striking headline. The useful question is: out of how many swims? Without the number of people in the water, a count of attacks says nothing about anyone's own risk.

Flying · The world and Australia

Flying, the safest way to travel

A commercial flight is one of the safest activities on this page. The risk halves roughly every ten years, and your choice of airline matters far more than the fear you feel on board.

The taxi sum

Beyond 5 km, the road to the airport is riskier than the flight

  1. A flight on a tier 1 airline: a 1 in 80 million chance, or 0.0125 micromorts.
  2. By car in Australia: about 2.5 deaths per billion kilometres, or 0.0025 micromorts per kilometre.
  3. Break-even point: 0.0125 ÷ 0.0025 = 5 km.
  4. Sydney CBD → airport: about 8 km, or 1.6 times the risk of the flight. From Manly or Parramatta, about 25 km, or 5 times.

What the sum assumes

The rate of 2.5 deaths per billion kilometres is a national average, close to the European figure (2.52). Sydney's urban roads are safer than country roads: the death rate per person is four times lower. Even if a city trip were half as risky, the break-even point would still be around 10 km.

The flight figure is a 2018-2022 average for airlines from the safest countries. Australian airlines do even better: no deaths on scheduled services since a turboprop crashed at Lockhart River in 2005. For a flight at the world average, it takes about 30 km of driving to match the risk.

The sum counts one trip. Add the taxi at the other end and the road's share doubles.

An older University of Michigan study (Sivak and Flannagan) reached the same conclusion with 1990s data: 18 km on a rural interstate, the safest kind of road, matched one nonstop flight on a major US airline.

September 11 and the roads

In the twelve months after the 2001 attacks, many Americans drove instead of flying. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer estimates that about 1,500 more people died on US roads during that period. How much of the shift came from avoiding planes is debated, but the mechanism is clear: running from a spectacular risk can lead you into a bigger one.

A bad year is still rare

2025 was a bad year: 394 deaths according to IATA, more than three quarters of them in two accidents, Air India 171 in Ahmedabad (260 deaths) and the Washington collision (67 deaths). Spread over about 5 billion passengers, that is still 1 death for nearly 13 million boardings. In 2023, IATA calculated that you would have to fly every day for 103,239 years to be in an accident that kills everyone on board.

Sharks · Sydney and Australia

Sharks in Sydney and across Australia

Australia records about 20 unprovoked shark bites a year and two or three deaths; in 2025, that was a third of the world’s bites. Sydney has had a bad run, with three deaths in four years after 59 years with none. Per swim, the risk is still tiny, and far smaller than the risk of drowning on the same beaches.

The risk per trip into the water

To turn a number of attacks into a risk, you divide by exposure. About 16.6 million Australians visit the coast, 3.3 times a month on average: roughly 630 million visits a year. About 1.8 million surf, 46 sessions a year each.

18 January 2026

127 mm of rain, three bites in two days

That day, Sydney had its heaviest rain since 1988. Within 48 hours, bull sharks bit three people: at Nielsen Park (fatal), at Dee Why (a board bitten, the boy unhurt) and at North Steyne in Manly (a man badly injured).

That was no coincidence. Bull sharks move closer to beaches after more than 45 mm of rain, when the water is above 20 °C, especially a few hundred metres from river mouths (Smoothey et al., 2023). The risk is not spread evenly: it concentrates on days and places that can be predicted.

Nets and drones

Since 1937, nets have been set from September to April at 51 beaches between Newcastle and Wollongong. In 2024/25 they caught 223 animals, 24 of them target sharks; 67% of the animals caught died. A 2024 study (Huveneers et al.) found no measurable difference in bites between netted and unnetted beaches since the 2000s.

Since late September 2026, 73 drones patrol 97 beaches, including all 38 of Sydney's ocean beaches, on top of 305 "SMART" drumlines that let sharks be tagged and released offshore.

Drowning, the real danger at the beach

On Sydney’s coast, 101 people drowned in ten years, against two killed by sharks. Across Australia, 313 people drowned in 2025/26, 151 of them on the coast, against two or three shark deaths in a typical year. In 2024/25, none of the 154 people who drowned on Australia's coast were swimming between the red and yellow flags during patrol hours.

In NSW, 63% of coastal drowning victims were more than a kilometre from a lifesaving service, 40% were caught in a rip and 88% were men. The flags do not protect against sharks: in June 2026 a swimmer was bitten at Coogee, less than 30 m from shore and between the flags. But they protect very well against drowning.

Everyday life · Australia

What actually kills Australians

Skin cancer kills about six Australians a day, the roads three or four. Sharks kill two or three a year. On the chart below, their bar is less than a pixel wide.

Sydney's sun

Australia has the highest melanoma rate in the world: 37 new cases per 100,000 people in 2022, ahead of Denmark and New Zealand. At least two in three Australians will be diagnosed with skin cancer in their lifetime. About 1,500 people die of melanoma each year, and about 800 of other skin cancers.

In summer, Sydney's UV index reaches 10 to 14; 11 and above counts as "extreme", and protection is advised from 3. In one summer week of 2023-2024, 15% of 15-to-24-year-olds reported sunburn.

The roads, a silent toll

1,314 people died on Australian roads in 2025, 355 of them in New South Wales. Nobody counts it as a disaster, because the deaths come one at a time, every day, everywhere.

In NSW, 68% of road deaths happen on country roads, where a third of the population lives. Speed is a factor in 38% of deaths.

Method and limits

How the numbers are calculated

  • Sharks, swimming. 9 swimmers killed in Australia from 2010 to 2025 (0.56 a year), divided by about 470 million swimmer visits a year: roughly 630 million coastal visits (Surf Life Saving Australia, 2025), 75% of them by swimmers as in the NSW survey. If swimmers only go in the water on one visit in two, the risk doubles. NSW alone gives a similar figure (3 deaths, 177 million swims a year).
  • Sharks, surfing. 21 board riders killed in Australia from 2010 to 2025, divided by 80 million sessions a year (1.76 million surfers, 45.7 sessions a year according to Lawes et al. 2023). With AusPlay’s participation figures, the risk would be about five times higher.
  • Surfing, all causes. 0.06 deaths per million hours of surfing (Lawes et al. 2023, Australia), times 1.88 hours per session.
  • Drowning. About 152 coastal drownings a year in Australia (2023 to 2026), divided by 630 million visits; NSW gives the same rate (56 a year for 238 million visits). Patrolled visits (51%) account for almost no deaths, so the risk outside patrolled areas is about twice the average.
  • Car. 0.50 deaths per 100 million vehicle-kilometres (BITRE), 1.57 occupants per car, about two thirds of deaths among vehicle occupants: about 2.5 deaths per billion passenger-kilometres, close to the European figure.
  • Flying. Deaths per boarding from Barnett and Reig Torra (2024) for 2018-2022: 1 in 80 million (tiers 1 and 2), 1 in 13.7 million (world), 1 in 2.2 million (tier 3).
  • Daily benchmarks. Annual deaths divided by 27.9 million people (ABS, March 2026), then by 365. These are averages across all ages: a thirty-year-old's all-cause risk is far below 18 micromorts a day.
  • Limits. These are population averages, not personal risks. Odds ratios are read as relative risks, which is reasonable for rare events. The share of bites by time of day does not account for how many people are in the water. Some figures come from secondary sources, flagged below. Data current to October 2026.
